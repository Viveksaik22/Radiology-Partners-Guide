Section 1

What Is Radiology?

Radiology is a branch of medicine that uses imaging technology to diagnose and treat disease. Radiologists — physicians who specialise in interpreting medical images — work behind the scenes in virtually every clinical pathway, from emergency trauma to routine cancer screening.

Modern radiology spans two broad domains: diagnostic radiology (using images to identify disease) and interventional radiology (using image guidance to perform minimally invasive procedures). Both rely on a shared technical infrastructure of modalities, networks, and software described throughout this reference.

Section 3

Components of an RVU (Relative Value Unit)

The Relative Value Unit (RVU) is the currency of physician reimbursement in the United States under the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (MPFS). Every CPT code is assigned RVUs across three components. Total payment is calculated as:

Payment = [(wRVU × Work GPCI) + (PE RVU × PE GPCI) + (MP RVU × MP GPCI)] × Conversion Factor

Component Full Name What It Measures Typical Share
wRVU Work RVU Reflects the physician's time, mental effort, technical skill, stress, and judgment required to perform a service. Determined by the AMA RVS Update Committee (RUC) and updated annually by CMS. ~52%
PE RVU Practice Expense RVU Covers overhead costs associated with delivering the service — staff salaries, equipment depreciation, supplies, and facility costs. Split into facility (lower) and non-facility (higher) rates depending on where the service is performed. ~44%
MP RVU Malpractice RVU Accounts for the professional liability insurance cost associated with the service. Higher for procedural and interventional work, lower for interpretive services. ~4%

Geographic Practice Cost Index (GPCI)

Each RVU component is adjusted by a locality-specific GPCI to reflect regional cost differences. CMS publishes GPCI values for over 80 payment localities. A GPCI of 1.0 represents the national average; high-cost areas (e.g., Manhattan, San Francisco) have GPCIs above 1.0.

Conversion Factor (CF)

The Conversion Factor is a dollar-per-RVU multiplier set annually by CMS through the MPFS rulemaking process. For 2024, the CF was approximately $32.74. Congress can override scheduled CF reductions through legislation, which has occurred frequently.

RVU in Radiology

Context How RVUs Apply
Radiologist compensation Most private practices and hospital groups tie radiologist pay to wRVU productivity. A typical diagnostic radiologist generates 8,000–12,000 wRVUs per year.
Facility vs. non-facility PE RVUs differ based on site of service. Procedures done in a hospital (facility) have lower PE RVUs than the same procedure in an outpatient imaging center (non-facility).
Technical vs. professional component Many radiology CPT codes split into a Technical Component (TC — equipment, staff, supplies) and a Professional Component (PC — interpretation). Each has its own RVU values appended with modifier -TC or -26.
Global billing When the same entity owns both the equipment and employs the radiologist, the full (global) RVU is billed without splitting modifiers.

System Full Name Role in the Radiology Workflow Key Standards / Tools
EHR Electronic Health Record The physician's primary system. The ordering provider enters clinical indication, selects the appropriate CPT/imaging order, and routes it electronically to the imaging center. The final radiology report is returned here for the referring provider to act on. Patient demographics, insurance, and prior results are also sourced from the EHR. HL7, FHIR, CCD/CDA
RIS Radiology Information System The operational hub of the imaging center. RIS receives the order from the EHR, schedules the patient, generates accession numbers, creates the technologist worklist, tracks exam status, stores the radiologist's report, and feeds charge data to billing. It communicates with PACS to link the report to the correct image set. HL7 ORM/ORU, DICOM MWL, IHE RWF
PACS Picture Archiving & Communication System Stores, retrieves, and distributes medical images in DICOM format. The imaging modality (MRI, CT, X-ray scanner) pushes images directly to PACS after acquisition. The radiologist pulls the exam from PACS onto a diagnostic workstation for interpretation. PACS also enables teleradiology — remote reading from any location. DICOM 3.0, IHE XDS-I, VNA
Modality / Scanner Imaging Equipment The physical device (MRI, CT, X-ray, ultrasound) that acquires the study. It receives patient demographics and protocol details from the RIS worklist (DICOM MWL), performs the exam, and pushes the resulting DICOM images to PACS. The technologist operates the modality and ensures image quality before sign-off. DICOM MWL, MPPS, DICOM Store
RCM Revenue Cycle Management Encompasses the full financial workflow from charge capture to payment posting. After the radiologist signs the report, the RIS triggers a charge with CPT and ICD-10 codes. The billing team (or automated system) scrubs the claim, submits it to the payer, works denials, posts payments, and manages patient responsibility. RVUs are assigned at this stage for physician productivity tracking. 837P/835 EDI, ICD-10, CPT, ANSI X12

Step-by-Step Breakdown

  1. Patient visits physician Physician evaluates the patient, identifies a clinical question (e.g., rule out fracture, evaluate lung nodule), and determines an imaging study is warranted.
  2. Order placed in EHR Physician selects the appropriate exam (CPT code), enters the clinical indication (ICD-10 diagnosis), and submits the order electronically. The EHR transmits an HL7 ORM message to the imaging center's RIS.
  3. RIS schedules the patient RIS receives the order, creates an accession number, schedules the appointment, sends patient prep instructions, and populates the technologist's DICOM worklist (MWL) with demographics and protocol.
  4. Patient arrives — technologist performs the scan The technologist confirms the patient's identity, reviews contraindications (metal, allergies, pregnancy), positions the patient, and acquires the study on the modality. Images are automatically pushed to PACS via DICOM Store SCU.
  5. Images stored in PACS PACS archives the DICOM images and links them to the accession number. The exam appears on the radiologist's worklist, prioritized by urgency (STAT, routine, wet read).
  6. Radiologist interprets & dictates The radiologist opens the exam in a diagnostic-grade PACS viewer, reviews prior comparisons, applies AI decision-support tools if available, and dictates the report using speech recognition software (e.g., PowerScribe, mModal).
  7. Report signed & returned to EHR The finalized report is signed in RIS, transmitted back to the referring physician's EHR via HL7 ORU message, and made available in the patient's chart. Critical results trigger immediate phone notification per ACR guidelines.
  8. Charge capture & claim submission (RCM) RIS sends CPT and ICD-10 codes to the billing system. The claim is scrubbed for errors, split into technical (TC) and professional (PC) components if needed, and submitted electronically to the payer via 837P EDI transaction.
  9. Payer adjudication & payment The insurance carrier adjudicates the claim, applies patient deductible/coinsurance, and remits payment via 835 ERA (Electronic Remittance Advice). Denials are worked by the RCM team; patient responsibility is billed separately.

Medicare, Medicaid & Insurance: How They Pay for Imaging

Radiology reimbursement flows from three primary payer categories — federal programs (Medicare, Medicaid) and private/commercial insurance. Each has its own fee schedule, coverage rules, and claim submission pathway.

5.2 — Medicare Coverage Rules for Imaging

  1. Medical Necessity (LCD / NCD) Medicare only pays for imaging that is medically necessary. Local Coverage Determinations (LCDs) issued by Medicare Administrative Contractors (MACs) define which ICD-10 diagnoses support payment for each CPT code.
  2. Appropriate Use Criteria (AUC) — CDSM Since 2023, ordering physicians must consult a CMS-approved Clinical Decision Support Mechanism (CDSM) before ordering advanced imaging (MRI, CT, PET, nuclear medicine). The consultation result is appended to the claim.
  3. Prior Authorization (Medicare Advantage) Traditional Medicare generally does not require prior auth for imaging, but Medicare Advantage plans may. CMS finalized rules limiting prior auth delays for medically necessary services starting 2026.
  4. Part B Deductible & Coinsurance Medicare Part B covers outpatient imaging. Patients pay the annual Part B deductible ($240 in 2024) plus 20% coinsurance after Medicare pays 80%. Medigap/supplemental plans may cover the 20%.

5.3 — Medicaid: State Variability

Factor Details
Fee Schedule Each state publishes its own Medicaid fee schedule. Some states pay as low as 40% of Medicare; others (e.g., New York, California) pay closer to 80–90% for certain services.
Managed Medicaid (MCO) Most states contract with private Managed Care Organizations (MCOs) to deliver Medicaid benefits. MCOs negotiate their own rates with imaging providers, which may differ from the state fee schedule.
Prior Authorization Medicaid and Medicaid MCOs frequently require prior authorization for advanced imaging (MRI, CT, PET). Turnaround times and criteria vary by state and plan.
Dual Eligibles Patients covered by both Medicare and Medicaid (dual eligibles). Medicare pays first; Medicaid may cover the remaining cost-sharing depending on the state's rules.

5.4 — Private Insurance: How Rates Are Set

Concept Explanation
Contracted Rate In-network providers sign a contract specifying payment rates, usually as a percentage of Medicare (e.g., 120% of MPFS) or a flat fee schedule. The provider must accept this rate as payment in full.
Allowed Amount The maximum the insurer will pay for a service. The provider writes off the difference between the billed charge and the allowed amount (contractual adjustment).
Deductible The amount the patient pays out of pocket before insurance begins to pay. Imaging costs count toward the deductible. High-deductible health plans (HDHPs) can result in patients owing the full contracted rate.
Coinsurance / Copay After the deductible is met, patients typically owe 10–30% coinsurance. Some plans use a flat copay (e.g., $75 for imaging). The insurer pays the remainder of the allowed amount.
Prior Authorization Commercial plans commonly require prior auth for MRI, CT, PET, and nuclear medicine. The ordering physician submits clinical documentation; the payer's radiologist or nurse reviews against clinical criteria (e.g., MCG, InterQual).
No Surprises Act (2022) Federal law limiting out-of-network balance billing for emergency services and certain scheduled services. Providers must deliver a good-faith cost estimate. Disputes resolved via Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR).

Section 7

Services at an Imaging Center

Modern imaging centers offer a full spectrum of diagnostic and interventional services, often under one roof.

Diagnostic Imaging

  • Routine X-rays and chest radiographs
  • MRI of brain, spine, joints, and abdomen
  • CT scanning with and without contrast
  • Ultrasound (abdominal, pelvic, vascular)
  • Echocardiography (cardiac ultrasound)
  • Nuclear medicine (bone scans, thyroid uptake)
  • PET/CT for oncology staging
  • Mammography and breast MRI
  • DEXA / bone density scanning

Interventional & Specialized

  • Image-guided biopsies (CT, ultrasound-guided)
  • Abscess drainage and fluid sampling
  • Tumor ablation (radiofrequency, microwave)
  • Vascular access and port placement
  • Uterine fibroid embolization (UFE)
  • Vertebroplasty / kyphoplasty
  • Peripheral artery and vein interventions
  • Fluoroscopy-guided joint injections
  • 3D post-processing and AI-assisted reads

How to Prepare for Your Imaging Appointment

Proper preparation ensures the best image quality and a smoother visit. Always follow instructions provided by your specific imaging center — these are general guidelines only.

  1. Confirm Your Order Bring a physician referral or imaging order, your insurance card, and a valid photo ID to every appointment.
  2. Review Fasting Requirements CT and PET scans often require 4–6 hours of fasting. MRI and plain X-ray typically do not. Confirm with the center in advance.
  3. Disclose Medications & Allergies Inform staff of all medications — especially blood thinners, metformin, and any previous contrast dye or iodine reactions.
  4. Remove Metal Objects Leave jewelry, piercings, and metal accessories at home. MRI patients must disclose all implants: pacemakers, stents, surgical hardware, cochlear implants.
  5. Wear Comfortable Clothing Loose, metal-free clothing is preferred. A gown may be provided depending on the body area being imaged.
  6. Arrange Transportation If sedation or contrast is used, arrange for a driver. Do not drive until cleared by the technologist or nurse.
  7. Hydrate (When Permitted) Good hydration aids contrast clearance and vein access. Ask whether drinking water is allowed before your specific exam.
  8. Arrive Early Arrive 15–20 minutes early to complete paperwork, answer screening questions, and change if needed — reducing delays for everyone.

Types of Radiology Professionals

A radiology encounter involves a team of credentialed professionals. Each role carries distinct educational requirements, certification bodies, and clinical scope.

5.1 — Physicians

Credential Title Description & Scope Certifying Body
MD Doctor of Medicine — Radiologist Allopathic physician who completed medical school, a 4-year diagnostic radiology residency, and often a fellowship (neuroradiology, IR, breast, etc.). Interprets all imaging, performs interventional procedures, and consults referring clinicians. ABR (American Board of Radiology)
DO Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine — Radiologist Osteopathic physician with identical radiology residency training and clinical privileges as an MD radiologist. Holds a DO degree but passes the same ABR boards and practices in the same scope. ABR / AOA

5.2 — Radiologic Technologists (RT)

Technologists operate imaging equipment, position patients, and produce diagnostic-quality images under physician supervision. All primary credentials are issued by the ARRT (American Registry of Radiologic Technologists).

Credential Title Description & Scope Certifying Body
RT(R) Registered Radiologic Technologist Primary credential for radiographers. Operates X-ray and fluoroscopic equipment, positions patients, and adjusts exposure parameters. Typically a 2-year accredited program plus national exam. ARRT
RT(MR) MRI Technologist Post-primary specialty in magnetic resonance imaging. Selects pulse sequences, manages contrast injection, and maintains patient safety in the MR environment (metal screening, implant clearance). ARRT (MR)
RT(CT) CT Technologist Post-primary specialty in computed tomography. Manages IV contrast protocols, gating techniques, and dose optimization strategies across body CT, cardiac CT, and CT angiography. ARRT (CT)
RT(M) Mammographer Post-primary specialty in mammographic imaging. Performs 2D and 3D digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) and breast spot compression views. May hold dual credentials with RT(R). ARRT (M)
RT(T) Radiation Therapist Delivers precision radiation treatments to cancer patients using linear accelerators (LINACs). Works under a radiation oncologist — in treatment, not diagnostic imaging. ARRT (T)
RT(N) Nuclear Medicine Technologist Post-primary ARRT pathway for nuclear medicine. Prepares and administers radiopharmaceuticals, operates SPECT and gamma cameras. Also credentialed separately by NMTCB as CNMT. ARRT (N) / NMTCB
RRA Registered Radiologist Assistant Advanced-practice radiologic technologist. Performs patient assessment, image-guided procedures, and fluoroscopy under radiologist supervision. Requires master's degree plus ARRT(R)(RA) certification. ARRT (RA)

5.3 — Sonographers

Credential Title Description & Scope Certifying Body
RDMS Registered Diagnostic Medical Sonographer Performs ultrasound of abdominal, pelvic, obstetric, and small-part structures. Credentialed by ARDMS with specialty exams (AB, OB/GYN, PS, BR, etc.). ARDMS
RVT Registered Vascular Technologist Performs non-invasive vascular studies using Doppler ultrasound — arterial, venous, DVT, cerebrovascular. Credentialed by ARDMS or CCI (RVS credential). ARDMS / CCI
RDCS Registered Diagnostic Cardiac Sonographer Performs echocardiography (transthoracic, transesophageal, stress echo) to assess cardiac structure and function. Specialty exams in AE, PE, FE via ARDMS. ARDMS

Mosaic Data Flow

How a study moves through the Mosaic platform from acquisition to billing:

  1. Study arrives — Modality pushes DICOM images to hospital PACS; RIS sends HL7 ORM order to Mosaic Router.
  2. AI triage — Mosaic AI layer runs configured algorithms on the incoming study within seconds of arrival; positive findings elevate priority automatically.
  3. Routing decision — Router evaluates radiologist pool: subspecialty match, state license, current workload, shift assignment → assigns to best-fit radiologist's Mosaic Worklist.
  4. Read & dictation — Radiologist opens study in PACS viewer (Mosaic-integrated or vendor PACS), dictates report using voice recognition (Nuance PowerScribe or Fluency); AI ambient tools suggest findings based on image analysis.
  5. Report sign-off — Signed report transmitted back to originating site via HL7 ORU; PACS status updated; critical result flag triggers call workflow if applicable.
  6. Charge capture — Mosaic RCM Integration auto-generates charge record with CPT, modifier, rendering NPI, and place of service; transmits to billing engine for 837P claim generation.
  7. Analytics update — wRVU, TAT, and charge data post to Mosaic Analytics in real time; site-level and radiologist-level dashboards refresh.

Section 11

When to Seek Imaging or Specialist Referral

Imaging studies are ordered by a licensed physician. The following are common clinical triggers that prompt a referral to an imaging center.

  • 🦴
    Unexplained Bone or Joint Pain Persistent pain not explained by injury — especially with night pain, swelling, or warmth — warrants imaging to rule out fractures, avascular necrosis, or bone tumors.
  • 🧠
    Neurological Symptoms New or worsening headaches, vision changes, vertigo, numbness, or focal weakness often require brain or spine MRI/CT to exclude stroke, mass, or demyelinating disease.
  • 🫁
    Chest or Pulmonary Concerns Persistent cough, hemoptysis, shortness of breath, or abnormal auscultation findings may prompt chest X-ray or CT for evaluation of pneumonia, effusion, or malignancy.
  • 🫀
    Abdominal or Pelvic Pain Unexplained pain, bloating, or mass may indicate gallstones, kidney stones, appendicitis, ovarian pathology, or other conditions visible on ultrasound or CT.
  • 🩸
    Cancer Screening & Surveillance Annual mammography, low-dose CT lung screening (LDCT) for high-risk smokers, and interval follow-up scans after cancer treatment are established indications.
  • 🏃
    Sports or Trauma Injuries Suspected fractures, ligament tears, meniscal injury, or rotator cuff tears require X-ray (acute) and MRI (soft tissue detail) to direct treatment or surgical planning.
  • 🔬
    Pre-Surgical Planning Surgeons commonly require detailed imaging before procedures to map anatomy, delineate tumor margins, and identify vascular supply — minimizing operative risk.
  • 📈
    Monitoring Known Conditions Chronic diseases — liver cirrhosis, aortic aneurysm, inflammatory bowel disease, known malignancy — require periodic imaging to track progression or treatment response.

Imaging Priority & Urgency Levels

Every imaging order carries a priority level that drives scheduling turnaround in the RIS and dictates how quickly the radiologist must read and report. Hospitals and outpatient centers use a standardized 4-tier framework aligned with ACR (American College of Radiology) guidelines.

STAT / Emergent (P1)

A STAT order is initiated when there is immediate threat to life or limb. The RIS flags the order in red and pages the on-call technologist immediately. Common STAT indications include suspected aortic dissection (CT Angio), acute stroke (CT/MRI brain), pulmonary embolism (CT PE protocol), tension pneumothorax (CXR), and intracranial hemorrhage. The radiologist issues a preliminary verbal or electronic report within 30 minutes, followed by a finalized dictation.

Critical Result Reporting

Regardless of original priority, any exam revealing a critical finding (e.g., pneumothorax, new intracranial mass, aortic aneurysm ≥ 5.5 cm) triggers a mandatory critical result communication workflow:

  1. Radiologist dictates preliminary report and calls the ordering provider directly.
  2. Communication is documented in the RIS with timestamp, recipient name, and callback number.
  3. If ordering provider is unreachable within 30 minutes, escalation to charge nurse or department head is mandatory.
  4. Final report in PACS/RIS includes a Critical Result Flag (CRF) tag for audit trail.
  5. Quality/compliance team reviews all CRF events monthly for ACR and Joint Commission accreditation.
